THE FINE PRINT, IN PLAIN ENGLISH

A model you can question.

This is a small experiment in interpreting evidence. Its assumptions are deliberately simple.

What exactly is being simulated?

Each film is an independent hit-or-miss draw. The opening round gives all four executives a hit probability of 50%. In the lab, Alex’s probability can rise to 80%; everyone else stays at 50%. “Ability” refers only to that assigned probability. Budgets, genres, collaborators, and market conditions are held outside the model.

What changes when I move a slider?

Within a history, each executive has a reproducible stream of random numbers. Adding films extends the same stream; changing Alex’s advantage changes the threshold for a hit. “Replay” draws a new history. The 1,000-history comparison uses separate random draws with the selected settings. A tie is counted separately, not awarded to an arbitrary executive.

What can—and can’t—I conclude?

You can investigate how track records vary under these assumptions and how sample size helps distinguish a true difference. You cannot conclude that real executives are equally skilled or that Hollywood success is entirely random. A result after several hits is not guaranteed to reverse: every new film is still an independent draw in this model.

How does this connect to communication?

The opening task invites a judgment before showing how the data was made. The reveal makes the limits of a short track record visible. The lab lets you compare observed outcomes with underlying probabilities. These design choices explore representativeness, overconfidence, framing, and selective attention from COMM 502. This prototype does not establish that users exhibit a bias or that it changes their understanding; that requires separate evaluation.
